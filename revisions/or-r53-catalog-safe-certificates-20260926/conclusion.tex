\section{Conclusion}
Selecting a finite command menu and allocating an accepted obligation can be optimized through the boundaries of the selected menu itself. Terminal-first allocation separates positive terminal layers from intermediate service. Each selected edge owns the histories whose eligibility ends there, and every path has exactly the capacity required for original-obligation repair. This identity yields a polynomial additive scheme for arbitrary eligibility while retaining exact common-prefix optimization as a stronger special case.

The guarantee preserves the original obligation, caps, realization ceilings, charges, and command budget. Its dependence on inverse normalized accuracy is polynomial; it is not an exact unrestricted algorithm or a relative-error approximation. A second capacity-conservative production model identifies the part of the argument that extends beyond renewal contracts. Catalog and threshold analysis distinguishes partition changes from genuine feasible-set changes.

The capacity potential shows that eligibility changes returns but not the conserved resource capacity. Anchor-envelope screening then removes certified non-anchor alternatives at their original targets and transfers global intervals across otherwise irrelevant refinements. Its equality case preserves an optimal decision without asserting that all optimal decisions are identical.

The theory and the synthetic evidence answer different questions. Exact certificates establish feasible decisions and valid global intervals. They do not establish that a small command interface is the principal implementation cost, that thresholds are accurately estimated, or that the method is the fastest optimizer. The retained numerical comparisons and explicit certificate costs make those distinctions operationally visible.
