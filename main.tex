{\Large\bfseries Neural Differential Utility:\\ Accepted Dynamic Service Contracts and\\ Certified Value-Gradient Learning\par}
\vspace{.3in}{Anonymous manuscript for Operations Research\par}\vspace{.25in}
\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
\textbf{Abstract.}
Can adaptive service contracts create value without reducing customer utility or service? We study externally priced protocols with a persistent tier, inventory decisions, and costly reconfiguration. An exact continuous-action analysis separates the accepted values of time, demand-regime, and inherited-tier information. In a reproducible instance, the deterministic optimum improves provider reward by 0.63666527 over the optimized continuous static contract while preserving customer utility, fill, and physical cost. Inherited-tier information contributes 0.01929038 of this gain. A primitive perturbation theorem explains customer-neutral improvement beyond the canonical instance. Allowing customer exit at every review leaves a certified gain of 0.45307876. Ordered envelopes yield switching regions and a quadratic discretization bound; an acceptance-aware energy identity and rational residual bounds certify approximate decisions. Equal-oracle learned value-gradient critics transfer from small training graphs to coupled portfolios with 1,024 tier coordinates. Their maximum certified loss per service is below one millionth, with classical solvers retained as accuracy and timing benchmarks. The results identify the operational sources of accepted adaptivity and give verifiable guarantees for its learned implementation.
\par\vspace{.16in}\textbf{Keywords:} service-level agreements; inventory control; persistent adjustment; customer participation; value of information; value-gradient learning.
\par\vspace{.10in}\textbf{Subject classifications:} Inventory/production: service contracts; Dynamic programming/optimal control: stochastic control; Analysis of algorithms: approximation guarantees.
\par\vspace{.10in}\textbf{Area of review:} Stochastic Models.

\section{Introduction}
A service provider can revise both physical capacity and the service tier attached to it. A higher tier may earn a premium and expose the provider to greater indemnity; replacing an installed tier consumes resources. Adaptation is valuable only if its gains survive the customer's commitments and comparison with a properly optimized static alternative. The central question of this paper is therefore operational: when can an accepted adaptive protocol create value without lowering service or customer utility, which information creates that value, and how can an approximate implementation be audited?

We study Neural Differential Utility (NDU) as joint control of physical activity and a persistent, externally priced contractual state. The tariff and accounting rule are fixed before optimization. This is an ordinary Markov decision process with an expanded state and action space, not a way to escape conventional stochastic control. The distinctive comparison holds the customer's utility, expected fill, and physical resource cost at the levels of an optimized continuous static contract. A common retainer, when present, changes participation levels but not the reported incremental provider rewards.

The institution is an observable, metered service protocol. A provider and customer agree on the tariff, the rule governing future revisions, and the required service before operations begin. This is a useful abstraction for auditable capacity or managed-service schedules, not a claim that all real service agreements make actions observable. We consequently also solve an extension in which the customer may leave after every review. There is no private type or hidden effort in either model; the extension relaxes continued customer commitment while leaving the tariff unchanged.

Our first contribution is an exact accepted information-value decomposition over continuous contract actions. A supporting-line argument identifies physical stock from the service and cost commitments themselves. The remaining acceptance constraint is a payment budget. Separate quadratic programs solve static, time-only, and time--regime rules; a verified Riccati recursion solves the full inherited-state problem. Every optimum is attained deterministically, including against public randomized protocols. In the canonical instance, the total gain is 0.63666527. Demand-regime information generates most of it; inherited-tier information adds 0.01929038 after regime information is already available. These are accepted values, not increments borrowed from an unconstrained provider calculation.

The second contribution explains persistence and robustness. Contract-specific primitive conditions give monotone decisions, ordered value envelopes, and a second-order tier-grid bound without assuming global concavity. We characterize complete multi-tier inaction bands rather than only pairwise switching thresholds. A customer-neutral perturbation theorem explains why concentrating a fixed expected payment budget on higher-payment regimes can reduce maintenance enough to cover adjustment. A separate customer-exit program retains a gain of 0.45307876 while satisfying every continuation-participation constraint. These results identify a mechanism and a commitment cost, not just an isolated favorable simulation.

The third contribution certifies approximate implementation of the accepted problem. A loss identity combines adjustment energy with customer-budget slack. Its quadratic residual counterpart verifies rounded continuous-action decisions using rational arithmetic. We train compatible scalar critics on coupled service portfolios, give value-only and derivative-weighted fits exactly the same raw oracle observations, and deploy without retraining on much larger graphs. The learned representation supplies accepted decisions with small certified losses and a measured repeated-solve tradeoff. Sparse and dense direct solvers, conjugate gradients, and an analytic polynomial filter remain in the comparison; the experiment does not require learning to dominate numerical linear algebra.

The paper follows this single sequence: institution, structure, accepted information values, and certified implementation. The main appendix and electronic companion retain the original physical-cost accounting, constructive neural approximation, continuous-review model, stopped-diffusion verification theory, and all earlier numerical evidence. The continuous-rate model has different adjustment units and remains a related extension; it is not used to validate the accepted jump-contract result. Nothing in the new exact certificate converts historical sampled neural diagnostics into continuum guarantees.

\subsection{Related literature and the incremental contribution}
Inventory decisions and service contracts have long been studied jointly. \citet{Henig1997} embed an inventory model in supply-contract design, connecting contracted quantities, delivery, and replenishment decisions. \citet{LiangAtkins2013}, \citet{Sieke2012}, and \citet{Katok2008} examine inventory agreements, penalties, coordination, and review periods. Our contribution is not the observation that contract terms affect stock. We fix the externally priced menu, retain the installed tier as a costly state, and compare accepted contingent revisions with an optimized continuous static tier under the same customer commitments.

Dynamic responses under service-level agreements are also established. \citet{Hosseinifard2022} optimize dynamic stocking under ready-rate agreements. \citet{Caggiano2007} address multiechelon service-parts agreements, and \citet{Abbasi2022} analyze service levels in vendor-managed inventory. Here even the static-contract comparator can adjust stock with the regime. The incremental controls are contractual tier revisions and their information requirements, not access to a state-aware inventory policy denied to the benchmark.

\citet{NasserTurcic2019} analyze temporary contract adjustment when a retailer has a private demand forecast. Their information rents and revelation problem differ from our public-state, fixed-tariff protocol. \citet{PlambeckZenios2003} study incentive-efficient control with unobserved effort. We do not claim to solve those incentive constraints: our customer-exit extension imposes continuation participation, not private-information truth telling. Combined pricing and inventory optimization is likewise established \citep{FedergruenHeching1999}; demand here is exogenous rather than shifted by the chosen price.

Our monotone selections use \citet{Topkis1978}; hysteretic control has predecessors including \citet{HippHolzbaur1988}. Ordered hulls, occupation measures, Lagrangian bounds, and completion of squares are standard tools. The new content is the contract-specific stock-identification argument, accepted information decomposition, primitive customer-neutral improvement, continuation-participation comparison, and acceptance-aware audit. The scalar Bregman identity is not advertised as a fast general multidimensional hull algorithm.

Derivative-weighted fitting follows Sobolev training \citep{Czarnecki2017}, and polynomial graph filtering follows \citet{Defferrard2016}. Our graph critic has a learned quadratic readout, not a newly invented convolution architecture. The contributions are equal raw-oracle access, larger accepted deployments, and exact post-deployment bounds. Forward--backward value-process methods \citep{MaProtterYong1994,HanJentzenE2018} and Markov-chain approximation \citep{KushnerDupuis2001} support the retained continuous-time material, which is labeled separately from the new continuous-action acceptance certificate.
